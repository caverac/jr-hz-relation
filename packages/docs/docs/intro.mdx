# 1. Introduction

**Goal of this section.** We state the two observations that this work explains, the
provenance bias of radial migration and the spiral $J_R^{\max}$-$h_Z$ relation. We then
introduce the one object that ties them together, the vertical form factor. Most of the 
discussion here is meant to be introductory, detailed explanations will come later.

## A disk galaxy as a collection of orbits

For the purposes of this work a disk galaxy such as the Milky Way is a large collection
of stars moving in a shared gravitational potential $\Phi$. Most of the visible mass sits
in a flattened disk, so the potential is close to axisymmetric and symmetric about the
midplane $z = 0$. A star in such a potential goes around the center at close to the
circular speed $V_c$, oscillates in radius about a guiding circle with the epicyclic
frequency $\kappa$ and oscillates through the midplane with the vertical frequency
$\nu_z$. Because the three motions are oscillations, the orbit is described by three
conserved actions, the angular momentum $L_z$, the radial action $J_R$ and the vertical
action $J_z$, with three angles that advance linearly in time. A vertically cold star has
a small $J_z$ and stays near the plane; a vertically hot one rises high above it. 

## Radial migration at corotation

Real disks grow spiral arms, the arms of a galaxy like ours are transient and
recurrent (Sellwood and Carlberg 1984; D'Onghia, Vogelsberger and Hernquist 2013; Baba,
Saitoh and Wada 2013). An arm is a rotating, non-axisymmetric perturbation of the
potential with a pattern speed $\Omega_p$ that differs from the orbital frequency
$\Omega$ of most stars, except at the corotation radius $R_{\rm CR}$ where
$\Omega(R_{\rm CR}) = \Omega_p$. There the arm does not rush past the star but hovers
nearly still in the star's frame, so the sustained torque exchanges angular momentum
with it. Since $L_z$ sets the radius of the guiding circle, the exchange moves the star
to a new radius while leaving its epicycle nearly untouched: this is radial migration
(Sellwood and Binney 2002; Roskar et al. 2008), the process that blurs the link between
the birth radius of a star and its present one.

## The puzzle: migration prefers vertically cold stars

In $N$-body simulations the stars that migrate are not a random sample of the disk.
Vera-Ciro, D'Onghia and Navarro (2016) named the effect the provenance bias and showed
that it, rather than vertical heating, sets the vertical structure of the migrators: the
strongest migrators are drawn from the kinematically cool population, with vertical
velocity dispersions below the local mean. Mikkola, McMillan and Hobbs (2020) confirmed
that the reach of migration in $J_z$ depends on the spiral strength and flagged the lack
of an analytic description. The usual explanation is that cold stars spend more of their
time near the midplane, where the arm's gravity is strongest, so they couple to it more
effectively. The sentence is right but it is not a derivation: it does not say how large
the bias is, how it depends on the thickness of the disk or on the strength of the arms,
or whether it connects to anything measurable. Turning it into a quantitative model is
the goal of this work.

## A second clue: the spiral $J_R^{\max}$-$h_Z$ relation

Palicio et al. (2025) measured, across simulated spiral galaxies and in the Milky Way,
that the maximum radial action reached by spiral-arm stars grows linearly with the
vertical scale height of the disk,

$$
J_R^{\max} = a\,h_Z + b , \qquad a \approx 3.7\times10^{-2}\,L_\odot\,\mathrm{kpc}^{-1} ,
$$

where $L_\odot = R_0 V_0$ is the angular momentum of the Sun, not its luminosity. The
slope was measured and not derived. We show in [Section 5](/results/provenance-bias)
that it follows from the same vertical structure that produces the provenance bias.

## The key object: a vertical form factor

A thin spiral arm has a potential that falls off above the plane as $e^{-k|z|}$, with
$k$ the radial wavenumber of the arm. A star feels the full midplane amplitude each time
it crosses $z = 0$ and a fraction $e^{-k|z|}$ of it at height $z$. The slow dynamics of
migration, however, respond to the average of the amplitude over a full vertical
oscillation, the vertical form factor

$$
F(J_z, k) = \big\langle e^{-k|z(\theta_z, J_z)|}\big\rangle_{\theta_z} ,
$$

which equals 1 for a midplane orbit and decreases with $J_z$. Section 3 shows that this
average is not a modeling choice: only the orbit-averaged part of the perturbation
survives to drive the corotation resonance, so $F$ is the coupling strength itself. It
is the thread of the argument, although not the sole determinant of either observable.
The size of the bias also needs the migration weight set by the resonance regime,
just as the slope needs the structural anisotropy of the disk. What $F$ supplies is the
link between them.

## The thread of the argument

1. [The vertical form factor](/theory/form-factor) defines $F$ and evaluates it on a
   realistic disk model (Section 2).
2. [The corotation-resonant Hamiltonian](/theory/corotation-hamiltonian) shows that $F$
   is the exact vertical Fourier coefficient of the spiral at corotation, reduces the
   resonance to a pendulum and finds that a single resonance floors the bias near
   $-7$ percent (Section 3).
3. [Resonance overlap and the diffusive regime](/theory/resonance-overlap) shows that the
   Milky Way sits at the overlap threshold of its bar and spiral, where trapping gives
   way to diffusion with a weight steeper in $F$ (Section 4).
4. [The provenance bias and the $J_R^{\max}$-$h_Z$ relation](/results/provenance-bias)
   states the dispersion offset per selection rule, derives the slope and predicts
   that the slope flattens with age (Section 5).
5. [The $N$-body test](/results/nbody-test) measures the model in a self-consistent disk
   of $10^8$ particles (Section 6).
6. The [Discussion](/discussion) places the result against the literature and lists the
   caveats (Section 7).

:::info A note on units
The vertical equations are written in units where $G = \sigma_z = z_0 = 1$, with $G$ the
gravitational constant, $\sigma_z$ the vertical velocity dispersion and $z_0$ the scale
height of the disk. The one number that survives the choice is the dimensionless
thickness $\alpha \equiv k z_0$, which is about 0.84 for the Milky Way.
:::

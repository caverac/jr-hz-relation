# 5. The provenance bias and the $J_R^{\max}$-$h_Z$ relation

**Goal of this section.** We now cash in the machinery. We first separate what the
Hamiltonian fixes, the scaling of the diffusion coefficient, from what it leaves to the
definition of a migrator, the dispersion offset per selection rule; we write a compact
closure for the diffusive regime and use it to predict the size of the bias and its
dependence on the thickness and on the spiral strength. We then derive the slope of the
empirical $J_R^{\max}$-$h_Z$ relation of Palicio et al. (2025) and find that it should
flatten with stellar age, a testable consequence.

## The diffusion coefficient and the selection rules

What the corotation Hamiltonian of [Section 3](/theory/corotation-hamiltonian) fixes,
once the disk is in the diffusive regime of [Section 4](/theory/resonance-overlap), is
the scaling $D_{L_z} \propto F^2$. A star that diffuses with that coefficient for a time
$\Delta t$ has a change $\Delta L_z$ of variance $V_1 F^2$, with $V_1$ the variance at
$F = 1$. The dispersion offset of a migrator population follows from it only together
with the rule that defines a migrator ([primer](/primer/diffusion)): a population
weighted by $|\Delta L_z|$ weights its stars by $F$, one weighted by $(\Delta L_z)^2$ by
$F^2$, while the stars with $|\Delta L_z|$ above a threshold $L_*$ carry the weight
$\mathrm{erfc}(\lambda/F)$ with $\lambda = L_*/\sqrt{2V_1}$. At $\alpha = 0.84$ the first
two give offsets of $-14$ and $-27$ percent, while the threshold rule is steeper than $F^2$
for a high percentile. [Section 6](/results/nbody-test) measures all three in a
self-consistent disk, where the offset is 5 to 9, 10 to 16 or 16 to 22 percent according
to the rule, each reproduced by the matching weight to within 0.05 in the dispersion ratio.

## A Boltzmann capture weight

A compact closure for the diffusive regime treats capture into migration as a
competition between the radial random energy $\sigma_R^2$ of a star (which carries it off
resonance) and the resonant coupling energy $|\Phi_s|\,F$, the spiral well reduced by the
vertical form factor. A Boltzmann capture probability
$\propto \exp(-\sigma_R^2/|\Phi_s|F)$ is the three-dimensional form of the random-energy
criterion of Daniel and Wyse (2015), whose in-plane criterion is the case $F = 1$.
Normalizing to a midplane orbit gives Equation (14) of the paper,

$$
W(J_z) = \exp\!\left[-s\left(\frac{1}{F(J_z, k)} - 1\right)\right],
\qquad s \equiv \frac{\sigma_R^2}{|\Phi_s|} ,
$$

with the single parameter $s$ controlling how sharp the selection is: a midplane star
has $W = 1$ and a vertically hot one, with $1/F > 1$, is exponentially suppressed. It is
the smooth, phenomenological counterpart of the crossover weight of Section 4 at
$S_0 \approx 2$ to 3, a closure rather than a consequence of the Hamiltonian.

At the Milky Way thickness the predicted bias spans $-20$ to $-41$ percent across the
physical strength range, covering the derived diffusive band of $-20$ to $-27$ percent
with no tuning. The closure makes two trends explicit: the bias weakens as the spiral
strengthens, since a smaller $s$ is a deeper well that captures hot stars too, matching
the strength dependence found by Mikkola et al. (2020); and it strengthens with the
thickness $\alpha$, independently of the spiral morphology, matching Vera-Ciro, D'Onghia
and Navarro (2016). The $-20$ to $-41$ percent band is the range of the closure over
$s$, not a measurement; the numbers to compare with a simulation are the offsets per
rule above.

## The $J_R^{\max}$-$h_Z$ slope

The same vertical structure links the bias to a different observable, the linear
relation $J_R^{\max} = a\,h_Z + b$ of Palicio et al. (2025). Two structural ingredients
give the slope. Vertically, the hydrostatic equilibrium of a self-gravitating
isothermal sheet between the pressure $\sigma_z^2$ and the gravity of a surface density
$\Sigma$ gives the scale height $h_Z = \sigma_z^2/(\pi G\Sigma)$. Radially, the epicyclic
action of a star with radial dispersion $\sigma_R$ and epicyclic frequency $\kappa$
scales as $\sigma_R^2/\kappa$, of which the hot tail that defines $J_R^{\max}$ is a fixed
multiple, $J_R^{\max} = c_{\max}\,\sigma_R^2/\kappa$ with $c_{\max}$ of order unity.
Dividing the two gives Equation (15) of the paper,

$$
a = \frac{J_R^{\max}}{h_Z}
= c_{\max}\,\frac{\pi G\Sigma}{\kappa}\left(\frac{\sigma_R}{\sigma_z}\right)^2 ,
$$

the squared radial-to-vertical heating-anisotropy ratio scaled by the structural
factor $\pi G\Sigma/\kappa$. A disk that is hotter radially than vertically reaches a
large $J_R^{\max}$ for a given $h_Z$, that is, a steep slope. For solar-neighborhood
values, $\Sigma = 50\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{pc}^{-2}$,
$\kappa = 0.037\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,pc^{-1}}$, $\sigma_R = 35$ and
$\sigma_z = 18\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, with $c_{\max} = 1$, the structural factor is a
velocity of $18\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, the anisotropy ratio is $(35/18)^2 = 3.78$, so that

$$
a \approx 69\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}} = 3.6\times10^{-2}\,L_\odot\,\mathrm{kpc}^{-1},
$$

within a few percent of the measured $3.7\times10^{-2}$. The dimensions are those of an
action per unit length, as $J_R^{\max}/h_Z$ must be.

:::caution $L_\odot$ is the angular momentum of the Sun
The relation is quoted in $L_\odot/\mathrm{kpc}$, where
$L_\odot \equiv R_0 V_0 \approx 1.9\times10^{3}\,\mathrm{kpc\,km\,s^{-1}}$ is the orbital
angular momentum of the Sun, an action, not the solar luminosity. Since $L_\odot$ is
a length times a velocity, $L_\odot/\mathrm{kpc}$ is a velocity, which is why
$3.6\times10^{-2}\,L_\odot\,\mathrm{kpc}^{-1} = 69\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. The engine computes
$a$ in $\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ and divides by $R_0 V_0$ only to report it in these units.
:::

One bookkeeping caveat: our $h_Z$ is the self-gravitating $\mathrm{sech}^2$ scale height,
whereas Palicio et al. (2025) fit an exponential vertical scale length; the two differ
by an order-unity factor that we absorb into $c_{\max}$. The slope is structural, not
the third decimal.

## The slope runs with age

In Equation (15) the surface density and the epicyclic frequency are structural,
properties of the disk at a given radius that are the same for old and young stars.
The only factor that depends on the population selected is the anisotropy ratio
$(\sigma_R/\sigma_z)^2$, in which each dispersion grows with stellar age through an
age-velocity-dispersion relation, $\sigma \propto \mathrm{age}^{\beta}$, with a different
exponent for the two channels. Hence Equation (16) of the paper,

$$
a(\mathrm{age}) \propto \mathrm{age}^{\,2(\beta_R - \beta_z)} .
$$

The measured exponents are $\beta_z \approx 0.5$ and $\beta_R \approx 0.35$ (Aumer,
Binney and Schoenrich 2016; Sun et al. 2025), so the exponent is
$2(0.35 - 0.50) = -0.3$ and an 8 Gyr population has a slope $8^{-0.3} = 0.54$ times that
of a 1 Gyr population: the $J_R^{\max}$-$h_Z$ relation should flatten with age.

This is a sharp, falsifiable test. Re-fitting the relation in mono-age or
mono-abundance bins of current Gaia, APOGEE and LAMOST data measures $\beta_R - \beta_z$
from spiral-arm kinematics; an age-independent slope would instead require
$\beta_R = \beta_z$, a different statement about how disks heat.

:::tip What to carry forward
One object, the vertical form factor, links the size of the provenance bias to the
slope of the $J_R^{\max}$-$h_Z$ relation through the same thickness $\alpha = k h_Z$.
Neither magnitude is set by $F$ alone: the bias needs the migration weight of the
resonance regime and the rule that defines a migrator, while the slope,
$3.6\times10^{-2}\,L_\odot\,\mathrm{kpc}^{-1}$ or $69\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, comes from the
structural factor $\pi G\Sigma/\kappa\,(\sigma_R/\sigma_z)^2$, with $F$ selecting the
cold migrators that populate $J_R^{\max}$. The slope should flatten with age. The
[$N$-body test](/results/nbody-test) measures the scaling, the offsets per rule, the
symmetry of capture and the state of the arrivals.
:::

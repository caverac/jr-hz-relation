# 7. Discussion

**Goal of this section.** We step back and see the whole argument as one structure,
distinguish it from a result it is often confused with, state the caveats that bound
the calculation and restate the prediction that could falsify it.

## One object, three faces

The thread running through every section is a single analytic object, the vertical
form factor $F(k h_Z)$, which ties together three things that looked unrelated.
$F$ is the common thread and not the sole determinant of each: the bias magnitude and
the slope each draw on their own additional physics.

1. The provenance bias. [Section 3](/theory/corotation-hamiltonian) showed that $F$ is
   the exact vertical Fourier coefficient of the spiral at corotation, Equation (5), so
   the pendulum well that traps migrating stars is deeper for cold stars by exactly
   $\sqrt{F}$. That is the origin of the bias at the single-resonance level; the
   observed magnitude additionally needs the regime physics of the next point.
2. The vertical selectivity of capture. [Section 4](/theory/resonance-overlap) showed
   that the same $F$ controls when resonances overlap, so cold stars enter the diffusive
   regime first and the weight steepens from $F^{1/2}$ toward $F^2$.
3. The spiral $J_R^{\max}$-$h_Z$ relation. $F$ depends on wavenumber and thickness only
   through $\alpha = k h_Z$, the thickness that anchors the relation of Palicio et al.
   (2025). [Section 5](/results/provenance-bias) derives the slope from the structural
   anisotropy factor $\pi G\Sigma/\kappa\,(\sigma_R/\sigma_z)^2$; the form factor does
   not enter the slope directly, its role being to select the cold-migrator population
   that defines $J_R^{\max}$ and to share the same thickness. The model predicts that
   the slope flattens with age.

What was already known going in: the radial twin of $F$, the in-plane selectivity of
Daniel and Wyse (2015), our $G$ factor; the $e^{-k|z|}$ fall-off of a thin spiral
(Binney and Tremaine 2008); and the bias itself, seen in simulations (Vera-Ciro,
D'Onghia and Navarro 2016; Mikkola et al. 2020). What was missing, what this work
supplies, is the vertical extension that turns the verbal "cold stars couple more
readily" into Equations (5) to (8) and connects it to the measured slope.

What the $N$-body disk of [Section 6](/results/nbody-test) adds is the measurement that
the extension is right where it can be checked. The variance of $\Delta L_z$ over 0.5 to
2 Gyr grows as $F^{1.9}$ to $F^{2.0}$ on average over 3 to 12 kpc, the rings collapse on
$F$ twice as well as on $|z|$, the dispersion of the migrators is reproduced per
selection rule to within 5 points, capture is symmetric in the sign of $\Delta L_z$ and
inside 9 kpc the arrivals keep their vertical action. The one thing the simulation
corrects is the kernel: the arms are 0.8 as thick as the disk, so only the form factor
built on the potential of a thick arm gives the exponent 2.

The form factor should not be conflated with the finite-thickness reduction factors of
classical density-wave theory (Toomre 1964), which weaken the collective response of a
self-gravitating disk to a density wave. $F$ is the orbit average of an externally
imposed spiral felt by a single star at fixed $J_z$, a test-particle coupling and not a
modification of the self-gravitating response. Both involve the vertical structure of
the disk, but they act on different objects.

## Selectivity is not the same as fate

A closely related result is easy to conflate with this one. Roskar et al. (2013)
showed, from the conservation of the vertical action, that when a star migrates outward
its scale height grows as

$$
\frac{h_{Z,f}}{h_{Z,i}} = \exp\!\left(\frac{\Delta R}{2 R_d}\right),
$$

with $R_d$ the scale length of the disk. That law governs the fate of a star after it
has migrated, what happens to its vertical structure once it lands. The form factor
governs the selectivity of capture, which stars are picked up to migrate at all. The two
are distinct and complementary, with only the latter producing the provenance bias. A
population is reshaped both by who migrates and by what migration does to them. This
work is entirely about the former.

The simulation of Section 6 measures both halves and settles which picture of the
post-migration state the framework is consistent with. Schoenrich and Binney (2009)
argued that outward migration contributes to disk thickening because migrators keep
their vertical velocity dispersion as they move to a weaker restoring force, in the
reading of Loebman et al. (2011) and Roskar et al. (2013). Loebman et al. (2011) then
invoked that picture for the thickening in their simulation without comparing inward
and outward migrators with the population at their destination. Minchev et al. (2012),
assuming instead the approximate conservation of the vertical action, found that
outward migrators cool and inward migrators heat, so that their effect on the local
disk must be assessed relative to the destination population, a result that Vera-Ciro
et al. (2014) matched in the disk used here, where the vertical dispersion of outward
migrators always decreases while that of inward migrators increases; Minchev, Chiappini and
Martig (2014) showed that a massive merger reverses the roles. In Section 6 the capture
bias is symmetric in direction, with a Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance of 0.02 to 0.12
between the $J_z$ distributions of inward and outward migrators, while the arrivals follow
$J_z$ conservation inside 9 kpc: inward migrators arrive 24 to 29 percent colder than
the stars already there and outward migrators 6 to 12 percent colder, the numbers that
the provenance ratio times $e^{\Delta R/4R_d}$ gives, whereas conservation of the
vertical energy would make outward migrators hotter than the locals. The framework is
therefore consistent with Minchev et al. (2012) and Vera-Ciro et al. (2014) and not with
the energy-conserving picture, so the fate law of Roskar et al. (2013), which assumes
the conservation of $J_z$, is the one to combine with it.

## The two regimes are two ends of one axis

The form factor distinguishes two dynamical regimes that are the two limits of a single
weight $W(J_z) = F^p$. Single-resonance trapping, $p = 1/2$, is the clean pendulum of
Section 3, which floors the bias near $-7$ percent, weaker with finite arm thickness.
Resonance-overlap diffusion, $p \to 2$, is the inhomogeneous Balescu-Lenard description
of secular evolution in tightly wound disks (Fouvry et al. 2015), whose linear-response
complement is the perturbed distribution function of Monari, Famaey and Siebert (2016).
It gives a near-parameter-free $-20$ to $-27$ percent. A fully rigorous treatment would
compute $W(J_z)$ as the phase-space trapped fraction across the overlap transition
rather than in its two limits, a separate calculation that we defer.

## Caveats

Several caveats bound the calculation.

- Finite arm thickness. The $e^{-k|z|}$ kernel is the razor-thin leading term; at the
  Galactic $\alpha \sim 1$ the finite-thickness softening of Equation (2) is an
  order-unity correction, one the $N$-body disk shows is not optional: the arms have a
  measured $\mathrm{sech}^2$ scale of 0.8 disk scale heights, so the razor-thin kernel
  underestimates the exponent of the variance by a factor of about 1.4 inside 8 kpc. The
  thickness of the Galactic arms is not measured in the same way, which leaves the
  softened $F$ of Section 2 with $h \sim 0.5$ to 1 as the range to carry.
- Pattern-speed separation. The overlap parameter of Equation (11) depends on the gap
  between the bar and spiral pattern speeds. The table of Section 4 shows that the
  threshold conclusion holds across the literature range of the bar, while a slower bar,
  a faster spiral or the recurrent transient spirals of the Milky Way only deepen the
  overlap.
- The magnitude of the bias depends on how migrators are selected. In the $N$-body disk
  the dispersion offset is 5 to 9 percent for migrators weighted by $|\Delta L_z|$, 10 to
  16 percent for $(\Delta L_z)^2$ and 16 to 22 percent for a threshold at the 90th
  percentile, each reproduced by the matching weight in $F$. A single number for "the"
  bias is not meaningful without the rule, which places the comparison with Vera-Ciro,
  D'Onghia and Navarro (2016) and Mikkola et al. (2020), who establish the cold-migrator
  preference without a dispersion offset per rule, at the level of the regime
  ordering: single-resonance trapping near $-7$ percent against the $F^{3/2}$ to $F^2$
  weights of the diffusive regime.
- The quantitative claims hold inside 9 kpc and for baselines up to 2 Gyr. Outside 9
  kpc the arrivals in the simulation are hotter than $J_z$ conservation predicts, by a
  gap that grows with time, where the exponent of the variance is also above 2.

## The clean, testable prediction

The strongest forward-looking result is the age running of the slope, Equation (16):
the $J_R^{\max}$-$h_Z$ relation should flatten with population age, with the exponent
$2(\beta_R - \beta_z) \approx -0.3$, so that an 8 Gyr population has roughly half the
slope of a 1 Gyr population. To our knowledge this is an untested and clean prediction
for current Gaia, APOGEE and LAMOST data: re-fit the relation in mono-age or
mono-abundance bins and read $\beta_R - \beta_z$ off spiral-arm kinematics.

## Reproducibility

Every number and figure on these pages is produced by the open-source engine, with a
full test suite ([Using the library](/library/usage)), whose equations the interactive
figures run in the browser. Nothing here is a fit to the simulations: the
analytic chain runs from Laplace's equation ([Section 2](/theory/form-factor)) to the
measurable slope ([Section 5](/results/provenance-bias)) with the spiral strength as
the only physical input, which lands inside its independently known range.

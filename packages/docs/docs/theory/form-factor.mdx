# 2. The vertical form factor

**Goal of this section.** We build the object $F(J_z, k)$ from scratch. We first show why
the gravity of a thin spiral falls off above the plane as $e^{-k|z|}$, then turn the
orbit average of that fall-off into an integral that can be computed, evaluate it on a
realistic disk model, and correct it for the finite thickness of real arms. An
interactive figure shows the average being taken along the orbit.

## The potential of a thin spiral above the plane

Above the disk there is almost no mass, so the potential $\Phi_s$ of the spiral obeys
Laplace's equation, $\nabla^2\Phi_s = 0$ for $z \neq 0$. A tightly wound spiral varies in
the radial direction like a wave of wavenumber $k$, locally $\Phi_s \propto e^{ikR}$ in
the plane. Treating a small patch of the disk as flat, with $x$ along the radial
direction and $z$ vertical, we write $\Phi_s(x, z) = f(z)\,e^{ikx}$, and Laplace's equation
becomes

$$
\frac{\partial^2 \Phi_s}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \Phi_s}{\partial z^2}
= \big(-k^2 + f''/f\big)\,\Phi_s = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f'' = k^2 f .
$$

The solutions are $f(z) \propto e^{\pm kz}$, and the requirement that the potential stay
finite far from the disk keeps only the decaying branch on each side:

$$
\Phi_s(x, z) = \Phi_s(x, 0)\,e^{-k|z|} .
$$

This is a standard result (Binney and Tremaine 2008): the more tightly wound the arm,
the faster its influence dies away above the plane. The exponential is why vertical
motion matters at all for the coupling of a star to an arm. If the potential did not
fall off with height, every star would feel the same amplitude whatever its vertical
excursion, and the vertical action would drop out of the problem.

:::note Why a larger $k$ decays faster
A spiral is a sequence of overdense and underdense lanes of alternating sign, spaced by
half a wavelength, $\lambda/2 = \pi/k$. Just above an arm the overdensity dominates
because it is the nearest mass. At a height comparable to the lane spacing the
neighboring lanes of opposite sign lie at nearly the same distance and their pulls
cancel, leaving an exponentially small net potential; a more tightly wound arm packs
the canceling lanes closer together, so the cancellation sets in lower. Equivalently, a
sinusoidal source of wavenumber $k$ has a single length scale, $1/k$, and Laplace's
equation introduces no other, so the field can only vary vertically on that scale.
:::

## The orbit average

A star oscillates vertically as $z(\theta_z; J_z)$, with the vertical angle
$\theta_z = \nu_z t$ advancing uniformly ([primer](/primer/orbits)). Over one oscillation
it samples a range of heights, and therefore a range of amplitudes $e^{-k|z|}$. The
coupling that governs the slow dynamics is the time average of the amplitude over the
orbit, and because the angle advances uniformly a time average is an angle average:

$$
F(J_z, k) \equiv \big\langle e^{-k|z|}\big\rangle_{\theta_z}
= \frac{1}{2\pi}\oint \mathrm{d}\theta_z\; e^{-k|z(\theta_z)|}
= \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T \mathrm{d}t\; e^{-k|z(t)|} ,
$$

with $T = 2\pi/\nu_z$ the vertical period. The function $z(t)$ is not known in closed
form, but the vertical velocity at each height follows from the conserved vertical
energy, $E_z = v_z^2/2 + \Phi(z)$, so that $v_z = \sqrt{2[E_z - \Phi(z)]}$ and the time
element can be traded for a height element, $\mathrm{d}t = \mathrm{d}z/|v_z|$. One period
consists of four identical quarter swings between the midplane and the turning point
$z_m$ where $\Phi(z_m) = E_z$, and since $e^{-k|z|}$ is even in $z$ the four contribute
equally. With $1/T = \nu_z/2\pi$ we obtain the working formula, Equation (1) of the
paper,

$$
F(J_z, k) = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{z_m} \mathrm{d}z\; e^{-kz}\,\frac{\nu_z}{|v_z|},
\qquad v_z = \sqrt{2[E_z - \Phi(z)]} .
$$

The integrand has a direct reading: $\nu_z/|v_z|$ is proportional to the time per unit
height that the star spends at $z$, long near the turning point and short through the
midplane, and $e^{-kz}$ weights each height by the reach of the arm. $F$ is the time
spent near the plane, weighted by the spiral's reach.

## Evaluation on the isothermal sheet

To obtain numbers we need a vertical potential. We use the self-consistent isothermal
sheet of Spitzer (1942), $\Phi(z) = 2\ln\cosh z$ in units $G = \sigma_z = z_0 = 1$, the
standard model of a stellar layer of a single velocity dispersion supported by its own
gravity. Everything follows from this one function. Near the plane
$\ln\cosh z \approx z^2/2$, so the potential is harmonic with
$\Phi''(0) = 2$ and the small-amplitude vertical frequency is $\sqrt{2}$; at higher
energy the orbit feels the flatter top of the well and $\nu_z$ drops. The turning point
is $z_m = \mathrm{arccosh}\,e^{E_z/2}$, and the vertical action
$J_z = (2/\pi)\int_0^{z_m}\mathrm{d}z\,\sqrt{2[E_z - \Phi(z)]}$ maps each energy to an
action, the abscissa of the figures below. With $\Phi$ fixed the only free parameter of
$F$ is the combination

$$
\alpha \equiv k z_0 ,
$$

the wavenumber of the arm in units of the scale height of the disk: thickness and
wavenumber appear together, as the dimensionless thickness $\alpha$.

Two features of the figure carry the argument. First, $F$ decreases monotonically with
$J_z$: a hotter star feels a weaker orbit-averaged arm, which is the quantitative
content of "cold stars couple more readily". For the Milky Way value $\alpha = 0.84$ the
orbit of energy $E_z = 1$, at the mean of the population, has $F = 0.58$, and the orbit
of $E_z = 3$ has $F = 0.36$. Second, the coupling is appreciably selective in $J_z$ for the
Milky Way. A radial wavelength $\lambda_R \sim 3\,\mathrm{kpc}$, that is
$k = 2\pi/\lambda_R \sim 2\,\mathrm{kpc}^{-1}$ (Reid et al. 2019), and a vertical scale
$z_0 \sim 0.4\,\mathrm{kpc}$ (Bland-Hawthorn and Gerhard 2016) give $\alpha \approx 0.84$,
of order unity. Had $\alpha$ been much smaller than 1, $F$ would be nearly flat across
the population and the coupling barely selective. Whether the selectivity produces an
observable bias, and how large, is not set by $F$ alone: it also depends on the weight
$W(F)$ relating the coupling to the probability of migrating, which the resonance
dynamics fix and which differs sharply between trapping and diffusion
([Sections 3](/theory/corotation-hamiltonian) and [4](/theory/resonance-overlap)).

## Arms of finite thickness

The $e^{-k|z|}$ law is the razor-thin limit, in which all the mass of the arm sits in the
plane and the kernel has a cusp at $z = 0$. A real arm is overdense disk material with a
vertical extent $h_s$ comparable to the scale height $z_0$, and smearing the thin-sheet
source over that thickness rounds the cusp (Toomre 1964). Take the vertical profile of
the arm to be a normalized exponential, $g(z) = (\beta/2)\,e^{-\beta|z|}$ with
$\beta \equiv 1/h_s$, and keep the in-plane pattern $\Sigma_0 e^{ikx}$, so that the density
of the arm is $\rho(x, z) = \Sigma_0 e^{ikx} g(z)$ and we solve Poisson's equation
$\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho$ for it. Writing $\Phi = e^{ikx}\psi(z)$ turns
$\partial_x^2$ into $-k^2$, exactly as above, and leaves a one-dimensional equation for
the vertical shape,

$$
\psi''(z) - k^2\,\psi(z) = 4\pi G\,\Sigma_0\,g(z) .
$$

The Green's function of $\partial_z^2 - k^2$ is $\mathcal{G}(z) = -e^{-k|z|}/2k$, the
razor-thin kernel itself, with the prefactor fixed by the unit jump that $\mathcal{G}'$
makes across $z = 0$. The potential of a thick arm is therefore the source convolved
with the thin-sheet kernel,

$$
\psi(z) \propto \int \mathrm{d}z'\; e^{-k|z-z'|}\,g(z')
= \frac{\beta\big(k\,e^{-\beta|z|} - \beta\,e^{-k|z|}\big)}{k^2 - \beta^2} ,
$$

where the convolution of two two-sided exponentials is elementary, splitting the
integral at $z' = 0$ and $z' = z$. Dividing by the value at the midplane,
$\beta/(k + \beta)$, gives the softened kernel

$$
K(z) = \frac{\beta\,e^{-k|z|} - k\,e^{-\beta|z|}}{\beta - k} ,
$$

which equals 1 at $z = 0$ and recovers $e^{-k|z|}$ as $h_s \to 0$. The orbit average is
linear in the $z$ dependence, and each exponential is itself a form factor at its own
wavenumber, $\langle e^{-k|z|}\rangle = F(J_z, \alpha)$ and
$\langle e^{-\beta|z|}\rangle = F(J_z, 1/h)$ with $h \equiv h_s/z_0$, so that

$$
F_{\rm soft}(J_z; \alpha, h) = \frac{F(J_z, \alpha) - \alpha h\,F(J_z, 1/h)}{1 - \alpha h} ,
$$

Equation (2) of the paper. It is a fixed linear combination of the bare form factor at
two wavenumbers, with the limits $F_{\rm soft} \to F$ as $h \to 0$ and $F_{\rm soft} \to 1$
as $h \to \infty$, when the star never escapes the arm. The slider $h$ of the interactive
figure applies it: at $E_z = 1$ and $\alpha = 0.84$ an arm 0.8 scale heights thick raises
$F$ from 0.58 to 0.83. We use $F_{\rm soft}$ in place of $F$ when we put in Milky Way
numbers, and it weakens the single-resonance bias by an order-unity factor
([Section 3](/theory/corotation-hamiltonian)).

The thickness is not a refinement that can be left out. In the $N$-body disk of
[Section 6](/results/nbody-test) the arms have a measured $\mathrm{sech}^2$ scale of
$z_a = 0.25$ kpc, 0.8 of the disk's $z_0$, and with the razor-thin $F$ the variance of the
angular-momentum change grows as $F^{0.9}$ to $F^{1.35}$ inside 8 kpc instead of the
predicted $F^2$. With the same convolution for the measured profile, a $\mathrm{sech}^2$
layer in place of the exponential, the exponent is 1.4 to 1.9 there and 2.0 to 2.8
outside, 1.9 to 2.0 on average. The razor-thin kernel underestimates the exponent by a
factor of about 1.4 because its cusp at $z = 0$ makes $F$ vary too fast across the
heights that most stars reach, which are all inside the arm.

:::tip What to carry forward
$F(J_z, k)$ is the orbit-averaged amplitude of the spiral that a star feels. It equals 1
for a midplane orbit, falls with the vertical action, depends on thickness and
wavenumber only through $\alpha = k z_0$, and softens toward 1 when the arm has a finite
thickness $h$. The [next section](/theory/corotation-hamiltonian) shows that this same
$F$ is the coupling that traps stars at corotation: it was never a free choice.
:::

# Orbits, actions and angles

**Goal of this page.** The numbered sections describe a star by three numbers, its
actions, and the whole argument of the paper rests on one of them, the vertical action
$J_z$. We build that description from the orbit of a star in a disk, with an
interactive orbit whose four views show what each action measures. A reader who is
comfortable with Chapter 3 of Binney and Tremaine (2008) can skip to the
[next page](/primer/resonance).

## Three oscillations

A star in an axisymmetric disk potential $\Phi(R, z)$ does three things at once. It
circles the center at the angular frequency $\Omega$, it oscillates in radius about a
guiding circle of radius $R_g$ at the epicyclic frequency $\kappa$, and it oscillates
through the midplane at the vertical frequency $\nu_z$. For orbits that stay close to a
circle and close to the plane the three motions decouple, because the potential near the
guiding circle is well approximated by a quadratic form in the radial offset
$X = R - R_g$ and in $z$. The frequencies follow from the derivatives of the potential:
with $\Omega^2 = (1/R)\,\mathrm{d}\Phi/\mathrm{d}R$ at the guiding radius,

$$
\kappa^2 = R\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega^2}{\mathrm{d}R} + 4\Omega^2 , \qquad
\nu_z^2 = \frac{\partial^2\Phi}{\partial z^2}\bigg|_{z=0} .
$$

For a flat rotation curve, $V_c$ constant and $\Omega = V_c/R$, the first relation gives
$\kappa = \sqrt{2}\,\Omega$. In the solar neighborhood $\Omega \approx 28$, $\kappa
\approx 37$ and $\nu_z \approx 70\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,kpc^{-1}}$ (Binney and Tremaine
2008), so a star completes about 1.3 radial and 2.5 vertical oscillations per orbit. The
vertical frequency is the highest because the disk is thin: the restoring force near the
plane is set by the local density, $\nu_z^2 = 4\pi G\rho_0$ by Poisson's equation, and
$\rho_0 \approx 0.1\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{pc}^{-3}$ gives the number above.

## Actions

Each oscillation has a conserved amplitude, and the natural measure of it is the area
that the motion sweeps in its own phase plane, the plane of the coordinate against its
conjugate momentum, divided by $2\pi$:

$$
J = \frac{1}{2\pi}\oint \mathrm{d}q\; p .
$$

For the radial epicycle of amplitude $a_R$, the loop in the $(X, v_R)$ plane is an
ellipse of semi-axes $a_R$ and $\kappa a_R$, so $J_R = \kappa a_R^2/2$. For the vertical
oscillation the loop in the $(z, v_z)$ plane is the curve of constant vertical energy
$E_z = v_z^2/2 + \Phi(z)$, and the action is

$$
J_z = \frac{1}{2\pi}\oint \mathrm{d}z\; v_z = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{z_m} \mathrm{d}z\,
\sqrt{2[E_z - \Phi(z)]} ,
$$

with $z_m$ the turning point where $\Phi(z_m) = E_z$. The angular momentum $L_z = R v_\phi$
is itself the action of the azimuthal motion, and it fixes the guiding radius through
$L_z = R_g V_c(R_g)$. The three actions of a disk star are therefore

| action | what it measures | partner frequency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $L_z$ | the radius of the guiding circle | $\Omega$ |
| $J_R$ | the size of the radial epicycle | $\kappa$ |
| $J_z$ | the size of the vertical oscillation | $\nu_z$ |

Two properties make actions the right variables for this problem. First, each action is
conserved in a steady potential, and it is an adiabatic invariant when the potential
changes slowly compared with the period of its oscillation; the vertical action of a
migrating star is the example that matters here, since a star that moves to a new radius
over many vertical periods keeps its $J_z$ while its $E_z$ changes (Section 6 measures
this in the simulation). Second, the conjugate variable of an action, its angle
$\theta$, advances at a constant rate, $\theta(t) = \theta_0 + \Omega_J t$ with
$\Omega_J = \partial H/\partial J$ the frequency of that oscillation. A perturbation
expanded in the angles is therefore expanded in oscillating exponentials, which is what
makes a resonance easy to identify ([next page](/primer/resonance)).

## The vertical oscillation in a real disk

The harmonic approximation $\nu_z^2 = \partial^2\Phi/\partial z^2$ holds only near the
plane. The paper uses the self-consistent isothermal sheet of Spitzer (1942), the
vertical structure of a layer of stars with a single velocity dispersion $\sigma_z$ that
supports itself against its own gravity. In units $G = \sigma_z = z_0 = 1$ its density
and potential are

$$
\rho(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\,\mathrm{sech}^2 z , \qquad \Phi(z) = 2\ln\cosh z ,
$$

so the restoring force $-\Phi'(z) = -2\tanh z$ is linear in $z$ near the plane and
saturates at $\pm 2$ beyond one scale height. The vertical frequency therefore falls
from $\sqrt{2}$ at small amplitude toward zero for very energetic orbits, which spend
most of their period in the flat part of the potential, and the turning point is
$z_m = \mathrm{arccosh}\,e^{E_z/2}$. The interactive figure above integrates this
potential; the orbit for $E_z = 1$ turns at $z_m = 1.08$ scale heights, and for
$E_z = 6$ at 3.69. Since $E_z$ and $J_z$ label the same family of orbits, we use whichever
is more convenient: $E_z$ in the quadratures and $J_z$ on the axes of the figures.

## Hot and cold

A population of stars has a distribution of vertical energies. For the isothermal sheet
the distribution function is $f(E_z) \propto e^{-E_z/\sigma_z^2}$, so in our units the
number of stars per unit vertical energy is the Boltzmann factor times the time an orbit
of that energy takes, $\mathrm{d}N/\mathrm{d}E_z \propto e^{-E_z}/\nu_z(E_z)$. Its mean
vertical energy is 1.11, and its vertical velocity dispersion is the $\sigma_z$ of the
units. A star is called vertically cold when its $J_z$ is small compared with the typical
value of the population, and vertically hot when it is large. The provenance bias of the
paper is a statement about this distribution: the migrators have a smaller mean vertical
energy than the parent population, and the paper quotes the effect as the ratio of the
two dispersions, $\sigma_z^{\rm mig}/\sigma_z^{\rm all} = \sqrt{\langle E_z\rangle_{\rm mig}/
\langle E_z\rangle_{\rm all}}$, or its logarithm.

:::tip What to carry forward
A disk star is three oscillations, each with a conserved action and a uniformly
advancing angle. The vertical action $J_z$ measures the area of the $(z, v_z)$ loop, it is
kept when the potential changes slowly, and the population is distributed in it as
$e^{-E_z}/\nu_z$. The paper asks how the strength with which a star feels a spiral arm
depends on $J_z$, and the [next page](/primer/resonance) shows where that strength
enters: in the depth of the pendulum well at corotation.
:::

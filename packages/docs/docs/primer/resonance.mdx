# Corotation and the pendulum

**Goal of this page.** A spiral arm exchanges angular momentum with the stars that orbit
at its own speed. The exchange is governed by the simplest nonlinear oscillator
there is, a pendulum. We derive the pendulum from the motion in the frame of the arm,
read the two kinds of motion off its phase portrait and map them back onto the disk,
where they are the horseshoe orbits of Sellwood and Binney (2002). The paper's claim is
that the depth of the pendulum well is proportional to the vertical form factor $F$,
which is why both the island width and the number of trapped stars scale as
$\sqrt{F}$.

## The frame of the arm

A steady $m$-armed spiral of pattern speed $\Omega_p$ is a perturbation $\Phi_s$ that
depends on the azimuth and on time only through the combination $\phi - \Omega_p t$. In
the frame that rotates with the pattern it is static, so the energy of a star in that
frame, the Jacobi integral $H_J = H_0 - \Omega_p L_z + \Phi_s$ with $H_0$ the energy in the
axisymmetric potential, is conserved. A star inside corotation has $\Omega > \Omega_p$
and overtakes the pattern; a star outside lags behind it. Only a star at the corotation
radius, where $\Omega(R_{\rm CR}) = \Omega_p$, sees the arm standing still, so only there
can the torque of the arm act in one direction for longer than an orbital period. This
is the resonance that migrates stars.

## From the orbit to the pendulum

Write the unperturbed motion in the actions and angles of the
[previous page](/primer/orbits). The phase of the star relative to the pattern,
$\theta_r = m(\theta_\phi - \Omega_p t) + \ldots$, advances at the rate
$m(\Omega - \Omega_p)$, which vanishes at corotation: it is the slow angle of the
problem. The radial and vertical angles keep advancing at $\kappa$ and $\nu_z$, tens of
Myr per cycle, so any part of $\Phi_s$ that depends on them delivers alternating pushes
that cancel over a few cycles. What survives is the average of $\Phi_s$ over the two fast
angles, $\Psi\cos\theta_r$, with the amplitude $\Psi$ evaluated at fixed actions;
[Section 3](/theory/corotation-hamiltonian) shows that this average factorizes into the
in-plane amplitude times a radial factor times the vertical form factor,
$\Psi = \Phi_0\,G(J_R)\,F(J_z,k)$.

The slow motion then involves one angle and its conjugate action, the offset
$p = L_z - L_{\rm CR}$ from the corotation circle. Expanding the axisymmetric part of
$H_J$ about corotation, the linear term in $p$ has the coefficient $\Omega - \Omega_p$,
which is zero there by definition, so the leading term is quadratic and the slow
Hamiltonian is

$$
\bar H(p, \theta_r) = \frac{1}{2}\,g\,p^2 + \Psi\cos\theta_r , \qquad
g \equiv \frac{\mathrm{d}\Omega}{\mathrm{d}L_z}\bigg|_{\rm CR} .
$$

This is the Hamiltonian of a pendulum: $p$ plays the momentum, $\theta_r$ the angle,
$1/g$ the mass and $\Psi$ the strength of gravity on the bob. For a flat or declining
rotation curve $\Omega$ decreases outward, so $g$ is negative, $g = -1/R_{\rm CR}^2$ for a
flat curve in units where $V_c = 1$. A negative mass turns the pendulum upside down: the
stable equilibrium sits where $\Psi\cos\theta_r$ is largest, at $\theta_r = 0$, which is
the inter-arm region on the corotation circle, the analog of the Lagrange points $L_4$
and $L_5$ of a bar (Binney and Tremaine 2008), while the unstable one sits on the arm at
$\theta_r = \pi$. The equations of motion are $\dot\theta_r = g p$ and
$\dot p = \Psi\sin\theta_r$.

## The phase portrait

The portrait of the pendulum in the $(\theta_r, p)$ plane has two kinds of curves. A
star with a large $|p|$ circulates: it drifts past the arm in one direction, forward
inside corotation and backward outside it, with the arm only modulating its drift. A star
near $p = 0$ librates: it rocks back and forth about the stable point and never passes
the arm. The boundary between the two, the separatrix, is the level curve through the
unstable point, $\bar H = -\Psi$ in the sign convention above, which solved for $p$ gives

$$
p_{\rm sep}(\theta_r) = 2\sqrt{\frac{\Psi}{|g|}}\;\left|\cos\frac{\theta_r}{2}\right| ,
$$

widest at the stable point. The half-width of the libration island is therefore
$\Delta p = 2\sqrt{\Psi/|g|}$, with small librations about the stable point at the
frequency $\sqrt{|g|\Psi}$. Both scale as the square root of the coupling, which is the
origin of the $\sqrt{F}$ of the paper.

## Back in the disk: horseshoe orbits

Mapping $\theta_r$ back to the azimuth $\phi = \theta_r/m$ in the pattern frame and $p$ to
the radial offset $\delta R = p/V_c$ of the guiding circle, a librating star traces a
horseshoe. It approaches an arm from inside, where it moves forward; the pull of the arm
ahead of it raises its angular momentum and lifts it outside corotation, where it drifts
backward; it recedes from the arm, crosses the inter-arm point and approaches the other
arm from outside, whose pull lowers its angular momentum and drops it back inside. The
figure below runs the pendulum for a few dozen stars and draws them in the disk.

_**Horseshoe orbits.** The same pendulum mapped onto the corotation
circle of a two-armed spiral, in the frame of the pattern. The island half-width in
radius is $\Delta R/R_{\rm CR} = 2\sqrt{\epsilon F}$ for a flat rotation curve, with
$\epsilon = |\Phi_s|/V_c^2$ the spiral strength ([Section 4](/theory/resonance-overlap)):
for $\epsilon = 0.02$ and a midplane star the dashed circles sit 28 percent inside and
outside corotation, with a libration taking $1/(m\sqrt{\epsilon F})$ orbital periods, 3.5
for these values, about 0.8 Gyr at the solar circle. Switching the frame selector to
the inertial frame turns the arms at $\Omega_p$ and sends every star around the center:
the stars inside corotation gain on the arms, the ones outside fall behind, so that the
horseshoe is the difference between the two motions seen from the pattern._

Migration is what happens when the arm does not last a full libration. A transient arm
that grows and fades within one libration period catches a star on the inner branch of
its horseshoe, carries it to the outer branch and disappears before it can come back:
the star is left on a new guiding circle, up to $2\Delta p$ away in angular momentum,
with its epicycle no larger than before (Sellwood and Binney 2002). Which stars are
carried is the question of the paper. The island captures the stars that fall inside the
separatrix, a band of width $2\Delta p \propto \sqrt{\Psi}$ around corotation. Since
$\Psi \propto F$, a vertically hot star sits in a narrower band. Counting the trapped
area gives the single-resonance weight $W \propto \sqrt{F}$ of
[Section 3](/theory/corotation-hamiltonian).

## Numbers for the Milky Way

The strength of the Galactic spiral is $\epsilon \sim 0.01$ to 0.03 (the density contrast
of the arms translated into a potential, see Section 4), so the island half-width is 20
to 35 percent of the corotation radius for a midplane star and $\sqrt{F}$ times that for
a hot one; at the Milky Way thickness a star with $E_z = 3$ has $F = 0.36$ and an island
60 percent as wide. These are large fractions of the radius, which is why a single
transient can move a star by kiloparsecs. It is also why the islands of two patterns can
overlap, the subject of the [next page](/primer/diffusion).

:::tip What to carry forward
At corotation the dynamics reduce to a pendulum whose well depth is the coupling
$\Psi \propto F$. Stars inside the separatrix librate on horseshoe orbits and are the
ones a transient arm can carry to a new radius; the island half-width is
$2\sqrt{\Psi/|g|} \propto \sqrt{F}$, so vertically cold stars are captured preferentially.
[Section 3](/theory/corotation-hamiltonian) turns the island into a trapped fraction
and a bias of about $-7$ percent. The [next page](/primer/diffusion) then shows what
happens when islands overlap.
:::

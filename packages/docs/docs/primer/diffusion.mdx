# From trapping to diffusion

**Goal of this page.** One resonance traps stars in a pendulum island. Two resonances
whose islands overlap destroy the regular libration and let a star wander between them.
Many transient arms then turn that wandering into a random walk in angular momentum. We
show the overlap on the stroboscopic map of two resonances, then the random walk of an
ensemble of stars, from which we derive the one fact the paper builds on: in the diffusive
regime the spread in $L_z$ grows with the square of the coupling, so the diffusion
coefficient is proportional to $F^2$ and not to $\sqrt{F}$.

## Two resonances

Take two patterns with different pattern speeds, the bar and a spiral, say. Each has its
own corotation radius and its own pendulum island, so that in the slow action $p$ the two
islands are centered at different values, separated by $\Delta p$ and of half-widths
$\Delta p_1$ and $\Delta p_2$. Chirikov (1979) gave the criterion for when the two can
no longer be treated separately: the islands overlap when the sum of their half-widths
exceeds their separation,

$$
S \equiv \frac{\Delta p_1 + \Delta p_2}{\Delta p} \ge 1 .
$$

Below the threshold each island keeps its librating stars, with invariant curves
between the islands that no star can cross. Above it the two separatrices, each
surrounded by a thin chaotic layer even in isolation, merge into one connected chaotic
region, so a star started in one island can reach the other. The criterion is a rule
of thumb, since the last invariant curve between two equal resonances breaks already at
about two thirds of the simple estimate (Chirikov 1979), but it captures the transition
to within that factor.

The figure below integrates the simplest system with two resonances, a pendulum driven
by a second wave of the same amplitude that moves at unit speed in $\theta$,

$$
H = -\frac{1}{2}\,p^2 + \Psi\left[\cos\theta + \cos(\theta + t)\right] ,
$$

whose resonances sit at $p = 0$ and $p = 1$ with half-widths $2\sqrt{\Psi}$ each, so that
$S = 4\sqrt{\Psi}$. Because the second wave makes the Hamiltonian time dependent, the
orbits are shown once per period of the drive, a stroboscopic map: a regular orbit then
appears as a curve, a chaotic one as a cloud of points.

## The random walk

Inside the chaotic region a star's angular momentum no longer oscillates about a fixed
value. Each passage near a resonance, or each transient arm that grows and fades, gives
it a kick $\delta L_z$ whose sign depends on the phase $\theta_r$ at which the arm finds
the star, a phase unrelated from one arm to the next. The kicks add like the steps
of a random walk. If an arm of coupling $\Psi$ acts for a time $\tau$ on a star at phase
$\theta_r$, the pendulum equation $\dot p = \Psi\sin\theta_r$ gives
$\delta L_z = \Psi\tau\sin\theta_r$, with mean zero and variance $(\Psi\tau)^2/2$ over a
uniform phase. After $n$ arms the mean is still zero and the variance is the sum,

$$
\mathrm{Var}(\Delta L_z) = \frac{n}{2}\,(\Psi\tau)^2 ,
$$

while the distribution of $\Delta L_z$ tends to a Gaussian by the central limit theorem,
whatever the shape of a single kick. The growth linear in $n$, or in time, is what
defines diffusion, with the coefficient of proportionality, the diffusion coefficient
$D_{L_z}$, carrying the square of the coupling.

## Why the square of the coupling

The two regimes weight the vertical action differently. The difference is the
reason the paper needs the diffusive regime. In the trapping regime the arm carries
whatever sits inside its island, so the number of migrators from a population counts
the island's area, proportional to the half-width $2\sqrt{\Psi/|g|}$: the weight is
$\sqrt{F}$. In the diffusive regime the arm kicks every star, so what distinguishes a
hot star from a cold one is the size of the kick, proportional to $\Psi$; the spread in
$L_z$ after many kicks grows as the variance, proportional to $\Psi^2$: the diffusion
coefficient scales as $F^2$. Whether such a star counts as a migrator then depends on
the rule: a population weighted by $|\Delta L_z|$ weights its stars by the root-mean-square
kick, $F$; one weighted by $(\Delta L_z)^2$ weights them by $F^2$; and one defined by a
threshold $|\Delta L_z| > L_*$ selects the fraction of a Gaussian of variance $V_1 F^2$
beyond $L_*$, the weight $\mathrm{erfc}(\lambda/F)$ with $\lambda = L_*/\sqrt{2V_1}$.
[Section 5](/results/provenance-bias) states these rules and
[Section 6](/results/nbody-test) measures all three in the simulation.

## The kinetic theory behind it

The random walk above is the content of the quasilinear theory of a disk with a
fluctuating spiral spectrum. The inhomogeneous Balescu-Lenard equation (Heyvaerts 2010;
Fouvry, Pichon and Magorrian 2015) gives the angular-momentum diffusion coefficient of
a star of actions $J$ as a sum over the resonances,

$$
D_{L_z}(J) = \pi\sum_m |\Psi_m(J)|^2\,C_m(\Omega) ,
$$

where $C_m$ is the power spectrum of the fluctuations at the resonant frequency and
$\Psi_m$ the same resonant coupling as in the pendulum. Since the coupling at corotation
carries the vertical form factor, $|\Psi_m|^2 \propto F^2$, which is the diffusive weight
of [Section 4](/theory/resonance-overlap). The pendulum and the random walk are the two
limits of one problem: an isolated resonance traps, overlapping resonances diffuse, with
the vertical form factor entering both, once to the power one half and once to the power
two.

:::tip What to carry forward
Overlapping resonances replace libration by a random walk in $L_z$ whose variance grows
linearly in time with a coefficient proportional to the square of the coupling,
$D_{L_z} \propto F^2$. The bias of the migrators then depends on how migrators are
counted, with weights $F$, $F^2$ or $\mathrm{erfc}(\lambda/F)$, all steeper than the
$\sqrt{F}$ of trapping. [Section 4](/theory/resonance-overlap) shows that the Milky Way
bar and spiral sit at the overlap threshold and that cold stars cross it first.
:::
